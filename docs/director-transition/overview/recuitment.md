# Recruitment Guidelines

Recruiting for tech team has some challenges but I'll share some of the things
that I think went _right_ and some of the things maybe, I did wrong.

Even though we had the second most number of applicants last year,
I still think that knowing how someone will actually be once they are on team
is hard to gauge just through an interview.

I think one of the common things that I found in general amongst CS majors
is that it's hard to do technical side projects on top normal school work.

For example, people will take hard classes like Operating Systems or Networking
or whatever else hard classes there are and sometimes you just have a quarter where
the workload is too hard to balance with Mayfest tech team.

This is of course, not accounting for recruiting (which a lot of tech team members have to do as well).

So, I hope some of the insights I have to share can help address some of these issues
and problems that maybe the team has faced in the past

## What Went Right

I think that framing our recruitment process as being **accessible** in terms
of making it clear that we do not expect a lot of past experience, keeps
the applicant pool a lot more interesting and diverse than specifically
asking and looking for people who example, know React or TypeScript.

Also asking about past project experience and asking questions to applicants
during interviews to go more in-depth into the project(s) that they said to
have worked on.

Asking about past project experience, either on the application or during the interview
is going to the best way to gauge

1) overall technical competence
2) willingness to learn
3) what they are generally curious in.

::note

Remember that especially for underclassmen, to be less picky with side projects
since they most likely won't have that much experience to begin with.

Though, I think it's fair to hold third and fourth years to a higher expectation
(within reason of course)
:::

I also played a lot of emphasis on "vibes". If I felt that someone didn't really
amaze me or had something that made me see them differently than other applicants,
then that brought them down a little bit for me.

For example, last year, it was nice, in my eyes at least, that some of our members
where members of a band, have worked on different parts of Dillo Day (through FMO)
and stuff like that (though more on this later).

### Weeding Out Opportuinst

This is one of the harding things to look out for. Of course, I think being on tech team
is honestly one of the most unique experience you can get out of being a CS major at
Northwestern and there are of course, going to be people who want to be a part of it
purely because of the "Resume boost" that it holds being on the team without having
a lot of interest in being in Mayfest.

There no "formula" for figuring out who these people are, but I think that

1) Vibe checking during the interview
2) Asking directly why they want to be a part of Mayfest
3) Asking what interest them about Mayfest
4) Have they even been to Dillo Day?

Are questions that should show you people's real intentions.

## What Went Wrong

Where things this past year might have gone wrong can be summarized too me as the following:

1) Making the team too big
2) Too wide of technical gaps
3) Too many interviews
4) Not having a good way of gauging commitment

And I'll break each of them down next

### Making the Team Too Big

The team this year consisted of around 10 people and was about even split between
upperclassmen and underclassmen. Now, I think that having more people was _okay_...

I think that especially when it comes to building Software, the pace at which your project
can slow down if you have _too_ many people working on that project.

And this is actually something that has studied, see [Brooks Law](https://medium.com/@alameerashraf/brooks-law-why-adding-people-to-late-projects-makes-things-worse-43cc65a695b9).
Because of so, I think there's some approaches to this:

1. Make a list of projects that the team WILL work on and finish for that year
2. Put that list in the application
3. Ask people which project(s) they would like to work on and **why**
4. Ask them again during the interview
5. That's the project that they work for the whole year.

Maybe this is a bit more restricting but you can view this as applying to a specific
team in tech team. This way, everyone doesn't come straight in as a "floater" and
they already know generally, what they will be working on.

You can even make design documents for the different projects that you outline in Step 1,
include as much detail as you can so that they can get a full sense of what the project
will look and work end-to-end.

**In this framework, it would make more sense to still keep the team around the same
size as last year**

Or, another way to approach this is to hire perhaps 3-5 people who _really know their stuff_
and are generally _okay_ Mayfest members. Ideally, this would be people who already are
comfortable with our tech stack(s) and wouldn't need _that_ much onboarding but it depends.

And when they all come in, they should all easily be able to start work and pushing code out.

I'm not sure to quite honest which is better, but I am much bigger fan of the first solution
than the second one.

### Too Wide of Technical Gaps

Since we market our team as being newbie accessible, that means we have people on the team
with years of React development experience, and people who only know basics, to barely just
starting to learn to code.

Now, this isn't a problem per-say but it does mean that you need some way of getting everyone
to a point where they can be an individual contributor and able to work independently without
a lot of "hand-holding".

This is why I created the two learning resources for learning the Mayfest tech stack.

I'm not sure how good these tutorials are because not many people actually finished them
last year (lol) but I think they could be a good starting point.

Not being super strict on completing the Mayfest learning handbook (as well as not making one for mobile development)
is why I think, it was hard for everyone to be equal IC last year.

### Too Many Interviews

I think that last year I did 20+ interviews, it was basically around 2-3 hours of interviews per week.

Simply put, I would cap the number of interviews to 15. It makes the final decision process easier
since there's not as many people to filter through.

### Not Having a Good Way to Gauge commitment

I think that this one of the hardest things to do. I don't really have a lot of solutions to offer for this one
because of course, people can just lie.

Perhaps, it might be wise to consider people who don't have as much going on outside Mayfest more.

Of course, this may seem trivial but I think I might have been a lot looser when it came to people
having a lot more than just Mayfest (because otherwise I would have been pretty hypocritical of me lol)

But yeah, I think consider that a lot of people can say that they balance things well
but that's a lot different than how they would be when they actually _have_ the role.

## What to Look For in People

Below is the mental checklist I ran through whenever I’m reading an application or was sitting across from someone in an interview. None of these are strict “yes/no” criteria, instead think of them more as sign-posts that tell you whether someone is likely to thrive on tech team rather than merely survive.

### Curiosity That Runs Past the Assignment

- Do they talk about all of the different "edges" of whatever project they’re describing? (basically do they go in-depth)
- When they talk about a class project, do they light up about the _weird bug_ they chased for three hours or the _one feature_ they snuck in after deadline because it just bothered them too much to leave out?  
- If you ask, “What did you wish you had more time to build?” and they actually have an answer, then that's a pretty good sign

### Willingness to Learn _and_ Unlearn

A lot of students show up ready to flex the one framework they know. I was more interested in people who can say, “I thought X was the right tool at first, but halfway in I realized Y was cleaner, so I switched.” That tiny anecdote signals:  

1. Technical humility  
2. A habit of self-reflection  
3. Less ego when a teammate suggests a better approach

### 3) Evidence of Following-Through

I’m not opposed to people having unfinished side projects, but I do want proof they can finish something occasionally:

- A deployed site even if it’s ugly is good
- A club event where they owned logistics _and_ retrospected what could improve next time  
- A GitHub history that isn’t just green squares in Week 1 of each quarter  

:::note

Again, first and second-years may have fewer shipped projects. In those cases, look for any pattern of finishing what they start so things like class assignments, hackathon demos, or non-tech commitments or things that they have done for clubs or research.

:::

### 4) Collaborative “Vibes”

Tech team lives or dies by how well we gel under deadline pressure. Pay attention to:  

- Whether they credit teammates unprompted when describing past work  
- How they handle disagreement in the mini-design brainstorm you toss into interviews  
- If they ask _us_ questions about team culture instead of only drilling into the tech stack

### 5) Genuine Interest in Mayfest

- Have they been to Dillo Day? What stuck out to them?  
- Can they tie their personal goals—creative, technical, whatever—to Mayfest’s mission?  
- Would they show up on the Lakefill at 6 a.m. to debug the app if something melts down? (You don’t have to _ask_ that, but try to feel it out!)

### Quick Red Flags

- **Buzzword resume padding.** If every project is a laundry list of frameworks with no story behind _why_ they used them, dig deeper.  
- **Solo-hero mentality.** “I rewrote our backend in one night” sounds epic until you realize the team found out via Git diff.  
- **Overcommitment deja-vu.** Anyone already juggling five other orgs, three side hustles, and two labs might _believe_ they can add tech team. Reality says otherwise.
